Landing in Canada with zero local credit history is a paperwork problem, not a character flaw. Every lender reads the same two credit files, Equifax and TransUnion, and both only track what happens on Canadian soil. This guide covers the routes to a first credit card that actually reports to those bureaus, the habits that build a score, and the traps that drain a newcomer's wallet.


## The one-paragraph version

Newcomers start with a thin or nonexistent Canadian credit file, but the door is open: all five big banks run newcomer programs that issue unsecured credit cards with no Canadian credit history required (limits up to $15,000 at RBC and Scotiabank, up to $5,000 unsecured at BMO). Where that route fails, secured cards like the Capital One Guaranteed Secured Mastercard ($0 annual fee) or the Neo Secured Mastercard ($50 minimum deposit) report to both bureaus. Expect 3 to 6 months of reported activity before a first score appears. Charge small purchases, pay the full balance every month, keep utilization under 30%, and skip prepaid cards: they never build credit.

## Why newcomers start at zero

Your home-country credit history does not travel with you. The bureaus only track accounts opened under your Canadian identity, so even a spotless decade of borrowing abroad counts for nothing here. "No file" means "unproven," not "risky," and lenders treat the two very differently.

Step zero is the **Social Insurance Number (SIN)**: a nine-digit number required before you can apply for any credit product in Canada. Permanent residents and work and study permit holders can apply at a Service Canada centre as soon as they arrive. Without it, payments cannot be attached to your file.

A related reality: newcomers are typically offered **small limits at first**. That is fine. A $1,000 limit used at 15% builds exactly the same payment history as a $15,000 limit used at 15%. The record of on-time payments matters, not the size of the line.

## Route 1: bank newcomer programs (the easiest route)

All five big banks run newcomer programs, and all five are still live. They relax the history requirement and underwrite on identity documents and income instead. Approval is never guaranteed, but this is the highest-probability route to an **unsecured** card (no deposit) in your first weeks.

- **RBC Newcomer Advantage:** no Canadian credit history required for permanent residents and international students who arrived within 12 months, or temporary foreign workers within 48 months. Limits advertised up to $15,000. Banking fees waived 12 months.
- **Scotiabank StartRight:** apply without a Canadian credit history; eligible clients can apply for a limit up to $15,000, partly using home-country history. Open to permanent residents within 5 years, international students, and foreign workers.
- **BMO NewStart:** no-fee cards with no credit history required; unsecured limit up to $5,000, with a secured option if the unsecured card is declined. For permanent residents and foreign workers who arrived within 5 years.
- **CIBC Welcome to Canada:** first Canadian credit card with no credit history required, plus a two-year annual fee rebate; student cards up to $2,000 for international students.
- **TD New to Canada Banking Package:** credit cards included, plus secured cards for those who do not qualify unsecured. Limits vary by eligibility.

Ask about the newcomer card when you open your account; bring your immigration document and passport. Since every bank underwrites differently, a "no" at one does not predict the outcome at another. One or two applications is reasonable; five in a week is not, since each leaves a hard inquiry on your file.

## Route 2: secured credit cards (the deposit-backed route)

If the unsecured route fails, a secured card accepts almost anyone: you post a refundable deposit that becomes your credit limit, use the card normally, and get the deposit back when you close the account in good standing. These report to the bureaus like any other card.

- **Capital One Guaranteed Secured Mastercard:** $0 annual fee; 29.9% purchase APR (21.9% in Quebec). Capital One markets it as guaranteed approval if you meet its four conditions: age of majority, legal authorization to have credit, no existing or pending Capital One account, and no recent Capital One account in bad standing. Security funds may be required. Reports to both bureaus.
- **Neo Secured Mastercard:** minimum deposit of $50, the lowest in the market; reports to both TransUnion and Equifax. A monthly fee applies (check the current plan at application). No minimum credit score required.
- **Home Trust Secured Visa:** $500 minimum deposit; $0 annual fee at 19.99% purchases, or $59 annual fee at 14.90% purchases. Reports to both bureaus. Not available in Quebec.

## Route 3: retail and store cards (the side-door route)

Store cards, such as the Canadian Tire Triangle Mastercard, are issued by retail banks with their own underwriting. Some approve thin files: the Triangle card's eligibility page states no minimum credit score. Treat this as a backup, not a plan A: approval is at the issuer's discretion, there is no published newcomer program, and a failed application still costs a hard inquiry. Apply once, not in parallel with four others.

## Route 4: authorized user or co-signer (the borrowed-ladder route)

A family member or friend with an established Canadian card can add you as an authorized user, or co-sign an application. Their account history then starts appearing on your file. The caveat is real: you get zero control over the primary cardholder's behaviour. Their missed payment is your missed payment; their maxed-out limit is your raised utilization. Only take this route with someone whose payment habits are visibly clean, and treat it as a bridge while your own card builds its own record.

## What NOT to do

- **Do not buy a prepaid card to build credit.** You are spending your own preloaded money, not borrowing, so the issuer has nothing to report. The Financial Consumer Agency of Canada warns that prepaid products claiming to improve your credit rating are false advertising.
- **Do not apply to five premium cards at once.** Each application triggers a hard inquiry. A cluster of them on a thin file looks like desperation, and a premium rewards card was never going to approve a zero-history file anyway.
- **Do not use payday lenders as a bridge.** They report nothing useful to your file and trap exactly the people who can least afford it. A declined application is a signal to try a secured card instead.
- **Do not close your first card once a better one arrives.** Account age helps your score. Keep it open with a tiny recurring charge while the new card does the daily work.
- **Do not max out a small limit "just to use it."** A $450 balance on a $500 limit reads as 90% utilization even if you pay it in full that month. Small, boring, fully repaid charges win.

## Traps: the costed version

1. **Carrying a balance on the secured card.** At 29.9%, a $500 balance carried for a year costs about $171.79 in interest. Your deposit is collateral, not a prepayment. The card only builds if the balance hits zero every month.
2. **Cash advances.** Interest starts the same day, with no grace period. A $200 advance held 60 days at 29.9% costs about $10.07 in interest, before any cash-advance fee.
3. **The minimum-payment illusion.** Paying only the minimum counts as on time but keeps the debt alive at 20% to 30% APR. Minimums are a damage-limiter for bad months, not a strategy.
4. **Monthly-fee credit builders.** Neo's Build membership runs $9.99 a month ($119.88 a year); KOHO's credit-building subscription is $7 to $10 a month for a tradeline that is not even a revolving credit line. That is the most expensive way to get what a $0-fee secured card provides.
5. **The "no credit check" pitch.** Fine for access, but the account may never report your good payments. Ask whether it reports to Equifax or TransUnion; if not, it is neutral for your file.

## Your 90-day action plan

**Days 1 to 7:** Apply for your SIN at a Service Canada centre. Open a chequing account at a bank with a live newcomer program (all five big banks qualify) and ask about the newcomer credit card in the same visit. Bring your immigration document and passport.

**Days 7 to 30:** If the unsecured newcomer card is approved, you are done. If declined, apply for one secured card the same week, ideally a $0-fee one, with a deposit you can lock up (often $200 to $500). Set up automatic payments and pay the full statement balance monthly.

**Days 30 to 90:** Put one or two small recurring purchases on the card (groceries, a transit pass), keep spending under 30% of the limit, and pay in full. Check your free score monthly through your bank's app or Borrowell to confirm the card is reporting. At day 90 you have a file; by month 6 you typically have a first score. One clean tradeline beats two shaky ones, so only add a second card if you genuinely need it.

## How the score builds: the worked example

Take a $500 secured card (your deposit equals your limit). You charge $60 in groceries each month and pay the full $60 when the statement arrives.

- **Utilization:** $60 / $500 = 12%. Under 30% is the guidance from the Financial Consumer Agency of Canada; under 10% is considered ideal.
- **Payment history:** 12 months of this produces 12 on-time payments reported to both bureaus. Payment history is roughly 35% of a score, the single largest factor.
- **Interest paid:** $0. The grace period means purchases cost nothing when the balance clears each month.

Putting $450 on the same card is 90% utilization, which can drag a young score down even when every payment is on time. Small charges are a feature, not a limitation.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long until a first score appears?**
Plan on 3 to 6 months of reported activity, with the first numeric score typically around month 6, usually landing in the fair range (560 to 659). Reaching "good" (660+) typically takes 1 to 2 years of steady payments and low utilization.

**Is a job needed to get a newcomer card?**
Not always. Newcomer programs weigh identity documents and sometimes income or deposits rather than demanding a Canadian pay stub. International students with no income routinely get student and newcomer cards with modest limits. Showing income or savings still improves approval odds and the limit offered.

**Does a home-country score transfer?**
No. Canadian bureaus only track Canadian accounts. Scotiabank's StartRight can consider home-country history when setting a limit, but it does not import your score. Everyone starts fresh.

**Does checking a personal credit score hurt it?**
No. Self-checks are soft inquiries with zero effect. Banks, Borrowell, and Credit Karma show free scores in their apps. Check monthly while building; it is how you confirm your card is actually reporting.
