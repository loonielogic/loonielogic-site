Forty-nine Canadian money terms, decoded in a sentence or two each. Every entry ends with links to the LoonieLogic guide or calculator that goes deep. Figures are checked against 2026 sources; the A-Z rail jumps straight to each letter.

## A

### Amortization period

The total length of time it takes to pay off your mortgage if you made no changes. The standard is 25 years; insured mortgages max out at 25 years, while uninsured mortgages with 20%+ down can stretch to 30 (first-time buyers got access to 30-year insured amortizations in 2024-2025 reforms). Longer amortization = lower payments, more interest paid overall.

→ Learn more: [House Affordability Calculator](/calculators/house-affordability), [Home Buyers' Plan guide](/learn/home-buyers-plan)

### Average (effective) tax rate

Your total tax bill divided by your total income. It is always lower than your marginal rate because the first dollars you earn are taxed at the lower brackets. Someone earning $100k in Ontario pays an average rate of about 22% even though their marginal rate is 31.48%.

→ Learn more: [Income Tax Calculator](/calculators/income-tax-calculator), [2026 Federal Tax Brackets guide](/learn/federal-tax-brackets-2026)

## B

### Basic personal amount (BPA)

The amount of income every Canadian can earn tax-free. For 2026 the federal BPA is $16,452 (verified 2026-09-27 vs CRA). Provinces add their own BPAs on top, so your real tax-free amount is higher. Non-refundable tax credits like the BPA are valued at the lowest federal rate, which is 14% in 2026.

→ Learn more: [2026 Federal Tax Brackets guide](/learn/federal-tax-brackets-2026), [Income Tax Calculator](/calculators/income-tax-calculator)

### Beneficiary

The person (or people) you name to receive an account or policy when you die. TFSAs, RRSPs, RRIFs, and life insurance policies with named beneficiaries pass directly to them, skipping probate. If you do not name one, the account goes through your estate, which costs time and probate fees.

→ Learn more: [RRSP guide](/learn/rrsp), [TFSA guide](/learn/tfsa)

## C

### Capital gain (and the inclusion rate)

The profit when you sell something like stocks, a rental property, or crypto for more than you paid. In Canada only **50% of the gain** is taxable, and that half gets added to your income and taxed at your marginal rate (verified 2026-09-28: the proposed increase to 66.67% was cancelled outright on March 21, 2025). Selling your principal residence is fully exempt, but you still have to report it.

→ Learn more: [2026 Federal Tax Brackets guide](/learn/federal-tax-brackets-2026)

### Carry forward

Unused room or deductions you push into a future year. RRSP room carries forward indefinitely. FHSA room carries forward only one year ($8,000 becomes $16,000 max in year two). Tax losses and charitable donations can also be carried forward under their own rules.

→ Learn more: [RRSP guide](/learn/rrsp), [FHSA guide](/learn/fhsa)

### CMHC insurance (mortgage default insurance)

Insurance you are required to buy if your down payment is under 20%. The premium is 2.80-4.00% of the mortgage amount (verified 2026-09-27), added to your loan, not paid upfront. It protects the lender, not you. Putting down 20% is the only way to avoid it. The down payment planner quantifies that tradeoff (about $22,600 on a $600k home).

→ Learn more: [House Affordability Calculator](/calculators/house-affordability), [Down Payment Planner](/calculators/down-payment-planner)

### Compound growth

Growth on your growth. Returns earn returns, so money accelerates the longer it sits. At 6% a year, $10,000 becomes about $32,000 in 20 years and $57,000 in 30. Fees work the same way in reverse: a 2% fee versus a 0.2% fee can erase hundreds of thousands over a career.

→ Learn more: [Compound Growth Calculator](/calculators/compound-growth), [TFSA guide](/learn/tfsa)

### Contribution room

The amount you are allowed to put into a registered account this year. TFSA and RRSP room accumulate from age 18 (or the year you became a tax resident); unused room carries forward. CRA tracks it, but their number lags, so always cross-check against your own records, especially after a withdrawal.

→ Learn more: [TFSA Over-Contribution Risk Checker](/calculators/tfsa-room-checker), [RRSP Room Calculator](/calculators/rrsp-room), [TFSA guide](/learn/tfsa), [RRSP guide](/learn/rrsp)

### CPP (Canada Pension Plan)

The mandatory public pension every working Canadian outside Quebec pays into. In 2026 the maximum monthly payment at 65 is $1,507.65, but the average new retiree gets about $877 (verified 2026-09-27 vs ESDC). You can start as early as 60 (reduced 0.6% per month) or as late as 70 (increased 0.7% per month). Quebec has its own plan, the QPP, with parallel rules.

→ Learn more: [CPP and OAS guide](/learn/cpp-oas), [Compound Growth Calculator](/calculators/compound-growth)

### Credit score

A three-digit number (300-900) that summarizes how risky you look to lenders. Equifax bands: 660+ is good, 760+ is excellent, and the 2026 Canadian average is about 679 (verified 2026-09-27). The score is built from five factors: payment history matters most. You can check your own score free without hurting it.

→ Learn more: [Credit Score guide](/learn/credit-score)

### Credit utilization

How much of your available credit you are using, as a percentage. Owing $1,500 on a $5,000 limit is 30% utilization. Keeping it under 30% (ideally under 10% before applying for anything big) is one of the fastest ways to protect your score. Paying the card down before the statement date counts.

→ Learn more: [Credit Score guide](/learn/credit-score)

## D

### Deduction (vs tax credit)

A deduction shrinks the income you are taxed on; a credit shrinks the tax you owe. An RRSP contribution is a deduction: at a 31.48% marginal rate, $1,000 in saves $315 in tax. A $1,000 non-refundable tax credit at the 14% lowest federal rate saves $140. Deductions are worth more the higher your income; credits are worth the same for everyone.

→ Learn more: [RRSP guide](/learn/rrsp), [2026 Federal Tax Brackets guide](/learn/federal-tax-brackets-2026)

### Defined benefit vs defined contribution pension

A **defined benefit** pension promises you a formula-based payout for life (e.g., 2% × years of service × best-5-years salary), common in government and union jobs. A **defined contribution** pension only promises the contributions; what you retire with depends on the markets. DB is the gold standard and increasingly rare; most private-sector workers are on their own with RRSPs and TFSAs.

→ Learn more: [RRSP guide](/learn/rrsp), [TFSA vs RRSP comparison](/compare/tfsa-vs-rrsp)

### Dividend tax credit

A credit that roughly offsets the tax a Canadian corporation already paid, so dividends from Canadian companies are taxed lighter than interest income. It is why holding Canadian dividend stocks in a non-registered account beats holding bonds there. The credit has federal and provincial pieces and comes in two tiers (eligible vs non-eligible dividends).

→ Learn more: [2026 Federal Tax Brackets guide](/learn/federal-tax-brackets-2026)

### Down payment

The cash you put toward a home purchase. Minimums in Canada: 5% on the first $500k, 10% on the portion above $500k up to $1.5M (verified 2026-09-27). Under 20% down means mandatory CMHC insurance. The 20% mark is the real milestone: it kills the insurance premium and unlocks 30-year amortizations.

→ Learn more: [Down Payment Planner](/calculators/down-payment-planner), [House Affordability Calculator](/calculators/house-affordability)

## E

### Emergency fund

Cash you can reach in a day, kept for job loss or surprise bills: not invested, not in your chequing account tempting you. The standard target is 3-6 months of essential expenses in a HISA. Build this before investing beyond an employer match.

→ Learn more: [Best Bank Accounts for Beginners guide](/compare/best-bank-accounts-beginners)

### Employer match (group RRSP / pension)

Free money with paperwork. Many employers match your group RRSP or pension contributions (often 3-5% of salary). A 100% match is an instant 100% return. Nothing in the markets competes. Always contribute enough to capture the full match before funding a personal TFSA or RRSP.

→ Learn more: [TFSA vs RRSP comparison](/compare/tfsa-vs-rrsp)

## F

### FHSA (First Home Savings Account)

The best account ever created for first-time buyers: contributions are deductible like an RRSP, and qualifying withdrawals are tax-free like a TFSA. Limits are $8,000 a year and $40,000 lifetime (verified 2026-09-27 vs CRA). You must be a first-time buyer to open one, and the account closes after 15 years or the year you turn 71.

→ Learn more: [FHSA guide](/learn/fhsa), [FHSA vs Home Buyers' Plan comparison](/compare/fhsa-vs-home-buyers-plan), [Down Payment Planner](/calculators/down-payment-planner)

### Fixed vs variable mortgage rate

**Fixed** locks your rate for the term (usually 5 years): predictable payments, higher starting rate. **Variable** moves with the Bank of Canada's policy rate: cheaper on average historically, but your payment can rise. With the stress test, you must qualify at the higher of your contract rate + 2% or 5.25% either way (verified 2026-09-27).

→ Learn more: [Rent vs Buy Calculator](/calculators/rent-vs-buy), [House Affordability Calculator](/calculators/house-affordability)

## G

### GDS and TDS ratios

The two affordability tests lenders run. **GDS** (gross debt service): housing costs ÷ gross income, max 39%. **TDS** (total debt service): housing + all other debts ÷ gross income, max 44%. The affordability calculator runs both plus the down-payment rules and hands you the lowest of the three caps.

→ Learn more: [House Affordability Calculator](/calculators/house-affordability)

### GIC (Guaranteed Investment Certificate)

A deposit that locks your money for a fixed term at a fixed rate, with your principal guaranteed (CDIC-insured at member institutions). The price of the guarantee is liquidity: cashing out early usually means losing the interest. Useful for money you need on a known date, like a down payment in 18 months.

→ Learn more: [Down Payment Planner](/calculators/down-payment-planner)

### GIS (Guaranteed Income Supplement)

A monthly top-up for low-income OAS recipients. In 2026 a single senior can receive up to about $1,123/month on top of OAS (verified 2026-09-27 vs ESDC). It is income-tested and clawed back fast, which is why TFSA withdrawals (not counted as income) beat RRSP withdrawals for GIS recipients.

→ Learn more: [CPP and OAS guide](/learn/cpp-oas), [TFSA vs RRSP comparison](/compare/tfsa-vs-rrsp)

## H

### Hard inquiry vs soft inquiry

A **hard inquiry** happens when a lender checks your credit because you applied for something: it can dip your score a few points and stays on file ~2 years. A **soft inquiry** is you checking your own score or a pre-approval estimate: zero effect. Rate-shopping mortgages in a short window counts as one inquiry.

→ Learn more: [Credit Score guide](/learn/credit-score)

### HBP (Home Buyers' Plan)

Lets first-time buyers borrow up to $60,000 from their own RRSP for a down payment, tax-free (verified 2026-09-27 vs CRA). The catch: you repay it over 15 years, and missed repayments get added to your taxable income. Withdrawals from 2026-2028 get a 5-year grace period before repayment starts (up from 2). FHSA-first is the better move for new savings at every income level.

→ Learn more: [Home Buyers' Plan guide](/learn/home-buyers-plan), [FHSA vs Home Buyers' Plan comparison](/compare/fhsa-vs-home-buyers-plan)

### HISA (High-Interest Savings Account)

A savings account paying a real rate (around 2-4% at online banks vs ~0.05% at the Big Six). No lock-in, CDIC-insured, and the right parking spot for emergency funds and short-term goals. Rates move with the Bank of Canada, so the "best" HISA changes, so check current tables, not old blog posts.

→ Learn more: [Best Bank Accounts for Beginners guide](/compare/best-bank-accounts-beginners)

## I

### Inflation

The slow leak in your money's value. At 2% inflation, $100 today buys what $82 buys in 10 years. It is why "safe" cash loses purchasing power and why the compound-growth calculator shows results in today's dollars: nominal growth that trails inflation is going backwards.

→ Learn more: [Compound Growth Calculator](/calculators/compound-growth)

## L

### Liquidity

How fast you can turn an asset into cash without losing value. A HISA is perfectly liquid; a GIC is not (until maturity); your house is deeply illiquid (weeks to sell, thousands in fees). Match liquidity to the goal: emergency fund = liquid, retirement = illiquidity is fine.

### Land transfer tax

A provincial tax on buying property, paid on closing day. Ontario charges up to 2.5% on a sliding scale; Toronto adds its own municipal tax on top, effectively doubling it (verified 2026-09-27). First-time buyers get rebates (up to $4,000 provincial + $4,475 Toronto). Budget 1.5-4% of the price for total closing costs.

→ Learn more: [Down Payment Planner](/calculators/down-payment-planner)

## M

### Marginal tax rate

The tax on your *next* dollar of income. Earn $100k in Ontario and your marginal rate is 31.48%, but only the dollars above each bracket threshold are taxed at that rate, not your whole income. This is the number that decides TFSA vs RRSP, whether overtime is worth it, and what a deduction saves you.

→ Learn more: [2026 Federal Tax Brackets guide](/learn/federal-tax-brackets-2026), [Income Tax Calculator](/calculators/income-tax-calculator), [TFSA vs RRSP comparison](/compare/tfsa-vs-rrsp)

### MER (management expense ratio)

The annual fee skimmed off a mutual fund or ETF, expressed as a percentage. A 2% MER on a fund earning 6% eats a third of your return; a 0.2% index ETF keeps almost all of it. Over 30 years that gap can cost six figures. Always check the MER before buying any fund.

→ Learn more: [Compound Growth Calculator](/calculators/compound-growth)

### Mortgage renewal

When your term ends (typically 5 years) and you re-negotiate the rate for the next term. You are not stuck with your lender: this is your best leverage point to shop around, and switching lenders at renewal usually costs nothing. Never accept the first renewal offer without comparing.

### Mortgage stress test

The federal rule that you must qualify at the higher of your contract rate + 2% or 5.25% (verified 2026-09-27). It applies to insured and uninsured mortgages alike. It does not change your actual payment. It just shrinks how much you are allowed to borrow.

→ Learn more: [House Affordability Calculator](/calculators/house-affordability)

## N

### NETFILE

CRA's system for filing your tax return online through certified software. Most Canadians must or should file electronically; paper returns are slower and error-prone. First-time filers who are newcomers may need to paper-file once before CRA sets up their online access.

→ Learn more: [First-Time Tax Filing guide](/learn/first-time-tax-filing)

### NOA (Notice of Assessment)

CRA's receipt after processing your return. It confirms your income, refund or balance owing, and, critically, your available RRSP contribution room for next year. Keep every NOA; lenders and the RRSP room tracker both start here.

→ Learn more: [RRSP Room Calculator](/calculators/rrsp-room), [First-Time Tax Filing guide](/learn/first-time-tax-filing)

### Non-registered account

A regular taxable investment account: no contribution limits, no special rules. You pay tax on interest, dividends, and capital gains as they happen (only 50% of gains are taxable). The right home for overflow savings after your TFSA, RRSP, and FHSA are full, and for money you might need before retirement.

→ Learn more: [TFSA guide](/learn/tfsa), [RRSP guide](/learn/rrsp)

## O

### OAS (Old Age Security)

The federal pension almost every Canadian 65+ gets, no work history required. In 2026 the max is $751.97/month at 65-74 and $827.17 at 75+ (verified 2026-09-27 vs ESDC). High earners give some back: the clawback (recovery tax) takes 15% of income above $95,323. You can defer to 70 for 0.6% more per month.

→ Learn more: [CPP and OAS guide](/learn/cpp-oas), [Compound Growth Calculator](/calculators/compound-growth)

### Over-contribution

Putting more into a registered account than your room allows. TFSA over-contributions are taxed at 1% per month on the highest excess until fixed (verified 2026-09-27 vs CRA): withdraw the excess immediately, then file form RC243 by June 30. RRSP over-contributions get a $2,000 lifetime buffer before the same 1%-a-month penalty kicks in.

→ Learn more: [TFSA Over-Contribution Fix guide](/learn/tfsa-overcontribution-fix), [TFSA Over-Contribution Risk Checker](/calculators/tfsa-room-checker), [RRSP Room Calculator](/calculators/rrsp-room)

## P

### Pension adjustment (PA)

The number on your T4 that shrinks next year's RRSP room to account for your workplace pension. Roughly: your employer's pension contributions reduce your 18%-of-income RRSP room dollar for dollar. It is why people with good pensions often have tiny RRSP room, and why the TFSA matters more for them.

→ Learn more: [RRSP guide](/learn/rrsp), [RRSP Room Calculator](/calculators/rrsp-room)

### Pension income splitting

At tax time, couples can shift up to 50% of eligible pension income (including RRIF withdrawals after 65) to the lower-income spouse, cutting the household's total tax. It is one of the few legal ways to split income in Canada, and a key reason the higher earner often holds the RRSP.

→ Learn more: [RRSP guide](/learn/rrsp), [TFSA vs RRSP comparison](/compare/tfsa-vs-rrsp)

### Principal residence exemption

The rule that makes selling your home tax-free in Canada: capital gains on your principal residence are fully exempt. You still have to report the sale on your return. It does not cover cottages, rentals, or second properties: those face the 50% inclusion rate.

## R

### Rebalancing

Selling a little of what grew and buying what lagged to keep your portfolio at its target mix (say 80% stocks / 20% bonds). Once or twice a year is plenty. Without it, a bull market quietly turns your balanced portfolio into an all-stock bet.

### RESP (Registered Education Savings Plan)

The account for a child's education, supercharged by free government money: the CESG adds 20% on the first $2,500 a year ($500/year, $7,200 lifetime per child), and the sweet spot is $209/month (verified 2026-09-27 vs ESDC). Low-income families get extra grants (CLB, A-CESG); Quebec adds QESI. Miss the age-16/17 contribution rules and the grants stop.

→ Learn more: [RESP guide](/learn/resp)

### RRIF (Registered Retirement Income Fund)

What your RRSP becomes by December 31 of the year you turn 71: you can no longer contribute, and must withdraw a CRA-mandated minimum each year (a percentage that rises with age). Withdrawals are fully taxable income, which is why RRIF withdrawals can trigger the OAS clawback.

→ Learn more: [RRSP guide](/learn/rrsp), [CPP and OAS guide](/learn/cpp-oas)

### RRSP (Registered Retirement Savings Plan)

Contributions are tax-deductible now, growth is tax-sheltered, withdrawals are taxed as income later. 2026 limit: 18% of earned income up to $33,810; deadline for the 2026 tax year is March 1, 2027 (verified 2026-09-27 vs CRA). Withdrawals before retirement face withholding tax (10/20/30% by amount) plus full income tax. Best for high earners expecting lower income in retirement.

→ Learn more: [RRSP guide](/learn/rrsp), [TFSA vs RRSP comparison](/compare/tfsa-vs-rrsp), [RRSP Room Calculator](/calculators/rrsp-room)

## S

### Spousal RRSP

An RRSP you contribute to in your spouse's name. You get the deduction at your (higher) marginal rate; they pay the tax on withdrawals in retirement: income splitting that actually works, subject to a 3-year attribution rule. One of the best tools when one spouse earns much more.

→ Learn more: [RRSP guide](/learn/rrsp), [TFSA vs RRSP comparison](/compare/tfsa-vs-rrsp)

## T

### TFSA (Tax-Free Savings Account)

Contributions are not deductible, but growth and withdrawals are completely tax-free, and withdrawn amounts restore your room the next January 1. 2026 limit: $7,000; lifetime max for someone eligible since 2009 is $109,000 (verified 2026-09-27 vs CRA). Holds cash, stocks, ETFs, GICs. "Savings account" is just the name. Best for lower earners, short-term goals, and anyone who values flexibility.

→ Learn more: [TFSA guide](/learn/tfsa), [TFSA vs RRSP comparison](/compare/tfsa-vs-rrsp), [TFSA Over-Contribution Risk Checker](/calculators/tfsa-room-checker)

## V

### Vesting

When employer-contributed pension or stock benefits officially become yours. A typical schedule vests over 2-3 years. Leave before then and you forfeit the employer's contributions (yours are always yours). Always check the vesting schedule before changing jobs.

## W

### Withholding tax (on withdrawals)

Tax taken off the top when money leaves a registered account. RRSP withdrawals: 10% up to $5,000, 20% to $15,000, 30% above (Quebec adds provincial withholding). It is not the final tax. It is a prepayment credited on your return; you may owe more or get some back at filing time. HBP and qualifying FHSA withdrawals have zero withholding.
