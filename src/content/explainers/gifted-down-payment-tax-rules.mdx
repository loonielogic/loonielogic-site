When parents hand over money for a down payment, the tax answer is the easy part: Canada has no gift tax, so the recipient owes nothing. The lender answer is the picky part: the money has to arrive with a paper trail proving it is really a gift. And the surprise usually lands on the giver, when selling investments to fund it creates an unplanned tax bill.


## The one-paragraph version

Canada does not tax cash gifts, so a gifted down payment is not income and does not go on the recipient's tax return. Lenders accept gifted down payments from immediate family (parents, grandparents, siblings) but require a signed gift letter stating the money is non-repayable, plus proof the funds actually transferred, plus roughly 90 days of bank statements tracing the down payment's history. Giving cash costs the donor nothing in tax, but selling investments to raise the cash triggers capital gains on the donor's side. A "gift" that is secretly a loan is mortgage fraud, not a paperwork shortcut.

## The tax answer up front: there is no gift tax in Canada

Canada has no gift tax and no annual limit on tax-free personal gifts. Any resident who receives a gift of any amount, from almost any source, does not have to include it in income. CRA's own statistical materials list "most gifts and inheritances" alongside lottery winnings and the Canada Child Benefit as non-taxable income. A $10,000 gift from a parent and a $200,000 gift from a grandparent get exactly the same treatment: none.

Two exceptions get confused with personal gifts, and neither applies here:

1. **Gifts from an employer** are taxable employment benefits, reported on a T4.
2. **Tips and gratuities tied to employment** are taxable income.

Cash from family toward a home purchase falls under neither. It is simply not income.

## How the recipient reports it: they do not

A cash gift does not go on the tax return. There is no line for it, no slip to attach, and no schedule to fill out. Do not put it on line 13000 (other income); that line is for taxable amounts.

One thing to track: income the gift earns afterward. If it sits in an account earning interest, or gets invested and produces dividends or capital gains, that income is taxable in the recipient's hands like any other income. The gift itself is tax-free; the returns on it are not.

### The attribution question: parent to adult child is fine

Attribution rules stop income splitting through family members. They apply to gifts made to a **spouse or common-law partner** (Income Tax Act s.74.1(1): income and capital gains attribute back) or to a **related minor child** (s.74.1(2): income, but not capital gains, attributes back until the child turns 18). Gifts to adult children, siblings, and other adult relatives sit outside the rules entirely. So when parents gift cash to an adult child for a down payment, attribution is not in play. As a second layer, cash parked in a home is not income-producing property, and principal-residence gains are sheltered by the principal residence exemption.

## The lender side: gift letters, eligible donors, and the paper trail

Tax law says the gift is free and clear. Mortgage underwriting says: prove it. Lenders have two worries: anti-money-laundering rules require them to trace large sums, and they need to know the down payment is not borrowed money, since borrowed money changes the borrower's debt picture.

### Who can give

Lenders and mortgage insurers generally accept gifts from **immediate family**: parents, grandparents, siblings, a child, or a legal guardian. Friends, distant relatives, and colleagues are generally not accepted. There is no cap on the amount: the entire down payment can be gifted.

### The gift letter

The gift letter is non-negotiable. Its standard elements (from the CMHC gift-letter form and insurer-lender equivalents):

- Donor's name, address, and phone number
- Borrower's name and the relationship between donor and borrower
- Gift amount and date given
- A statement that the gift is **non-repayable**, with no expectation of repayment, express or implied
- A statement that no part of the gift comes from a third party with an interest in the property (such as the seller)
- Donor's signature, plus the borrower's written acknowledgement of receipt

### The 90-day paper trail

Lenders typically ask for about 90 days of bank statements on the accounts holding the down payment, looking for how the money accumulated and for large unexplained deposits that might signal borrowed funds. A gift shows up as exactly that kind of lump-sum deposit, which is why the gift letter, the donor's bank statement showing the funds leaving their account, and the transfer receipt all matter. Gifts are not exempt from the paper trail; they get extra scrutiny within it.

Decide which account will hold the down payment early, park the funds there, and stop shuffling money between accounts. Arrange the gift letter while it is a five-minute favour, not in the final week before closing.

### Gifted deposits vs. gifted down payments

For pre-construction purchases, a gifted deposit gets the same treatment as a gifted down payment: gift letter plus deposit receipts, with the deposit counting toward the down payment documentation.

## The donor side: when giving creates a tax bill

Giving cash has no tax consequence for the donor. No tax when it leaves the parent's account, and no deduction either.

The bill arrives when the donor **sells investments to raise the cash**. Selling appreciated stock, mutual funds, or a rental property is a disposition for tax purposes, and the capital gain is the donor's taxable income at the 50% inclusion rate, even though every dollar goes to the child's down payment.

Gifting the asset itself instead of selling first does not help. Under Income Tax Act s.69(1), a non-arm's-length gift of capital property is deemed to happen at fair market value, so the donor is taxed on the gain up to market value whether or not any cash changed hands.

### Worked example: the $65,000 gift that cost the parent $3,706.25

Say the parents gift $65,000 for a down payment. The recipient's tax: **$0**.

Now say the parents funded it by selling shares:

- Sale proceeds (fair market value): $65,000
- Adjusted cost base: $40,000
- Capital gain: $65,000 minus $40,000 = **$25,000**
- Inclusion rate 50%, so taxable capital gain = **$12,500**
- Parent's combined marginal rate (Ontario, 2026 brackets): federal 20.5% + Ontario 9.15% = **29.65%**
- Donor's tax: $12,500 times 29.65% = **$3,706.25**

The parents gave $65,000 and owed $3,706.25 on top, an expense nobody budgeted when the plan was "help with the down payment." Gifting the shares directly would have produced the same $25,000 deemed gain.

## The "loan disguised as a gift" trap

Sometimes the plan is a wink: the money changes hands, everyone signs a gift letter, and there is a quiet understanding it gets paid back later. This is the most dangerous move in the whole process:

1. **It is mortgage fraud.** Signing a gift letter that states the money is non-repayable when repayment is expected is a false statement on a mortgage application, and misrepresenting the down payment source is treated as a criminal matter.
2. **It distorts the debt ratios.** Lenders qualify borrowers on gross and total debt service ratios. An undisclosed repayment obligation means the borrower qualified on numbers that were not true. Discovered during underwriting, the deal collapses; discovered after, the lender can call the loan.
3. **The parents lose protection.** A genuine gift with a proper letter protects everyone. An informal loan dressed as a gift leaves the parents with no enforceable repayment terms and the borrower with a fraud exposure.

There is a legitimate version: borrowing for a down payment is legal through certain lender and insurer programs, but the amount must be declared and its payments included in the total debt service ratio.

## Non-family and international donors: advice territory

Gifts from outside the immediate family sit in a grey zone. Most standard programs do not accept them; a few specialized insured programs do. If the only available donor is a friend, check with the lender or a broker before counting the money.

International donors add complexity on three fronts:

- **Tax:** Canada does not tax the receipt of a foreign cash gift, and the recipient reports nothing on the income tax return.
- **Reporting:** If gifted foreign funds push the recipient's specified foreign property above $100,000 in cost at any time during the year, a **T1135 Foreign Income Verification Statement** is required. The cost of gifted foreign property is its fair market value at receipt.
- **Paper trail:** Lenders scrutinize cross-border transfers for anti-money-laundering purposes, and their gift forms specifically ask whether the funds originated outside Canada. Keep currency conversion records with everything else.

Cross-border gifts combine tax, foreign-exchange, and lender rules that vary by country. Professional advice before moving the money is money well spent.

## Co-signed vs. gifted: different tools

A gift and a co-sign both involve family help, but they work in opposite ways:

- **Gift:** money moves with no repayment obligation. It raises the down payment, shrinks the mortgage, and leaves debt ratios untouched.
- **Co-sign:** no money moves. The co-signer guarantees the mortgage, becomes liable for the debt, and takes a credit hit if payments are missed.

The two can be combined: a gift for the down payment plus a co-signer for income qualification.

## Traps to avoid

1. **The fake gift.** A "gift" with a repayment understanding is mortgage fraud, and the signed letter saying otherwise is the evidence. Declare borrowed money honestly or do not borrow it.
2. **Undocumented cash.** Physical cash or transfers with no record leave the buyer unable to satisfy the paper trail. Every dollar needs a visible path from donor account to borrower account.
3. **The non-eligible donor.** A genuine gift from a friend or distant relative can still be rejected under the lender's donor rules. Confirm eligibility before counting the money.
4. **The donor's capital gains surprise.** Selling investments to fund a cash gift creates a taxable gain on the donor's return. Budget the tax or plan the funding differently.
5. **Foreign gift reporting confusion.** The gift itself is not taxable and not reported as income, but foreign-held property above the $100,000 cost threshold triggers T1135 filing. Different rule, different form, easy to miss.
