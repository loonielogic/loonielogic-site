Fraud is the number one crime against older Canadians, but it hits every age group, and reported losses topped $638 million in 2024. This guide covers the scams Canadians actually face, the red flags that give them away, and exactly what to do in the first 24 hours if one works on you.


## The one-paragraph version

Most scams in Canada follow one of a few scripts: a fake message that creates panic or excitement, a demand to act immediately, and a payment method you cannot reverse (gift cards, crypto, e-transfers, wire transfers). The best single defence is **independent verification**: never use the phone number, link, or email the stranger gave you. Look up the real contact yourself. If you do get scammed, speed matters: contact your bank, the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre (1-888-495-8501), and your local police right away, then lock down your credit files and passwords.

## How big is the problem?

The Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre (CAFC) publishes annual statistics. For 2024, the latest full year:

- **$638 million+ in reported losses**, up from $578 million in 2023 (RCMP).
- **108,878 fraud incidents** reported (CAFC, via money.ca).
- **34,621 reported victims** of fraud (CAFC, via RCMP).
- Investment scams were the costliest single category at **nearly $311 million**; spear phishing topped $67 million and romance scams topped $58 million.
- **Cyber-enabled fraud** (fraud carried out online) made up 75% of all dollar losses.
- The average reported loss per individual victim was **$15,028**.

These are only the reported numbers, and the CAFC estimates that just **5 to 10 percent** of victims ever report.

### Worked example: what the averages mean

Divide the headline numbers:

- $638,000,000 / 34,621 reported victims = **about $18,428 per reported victim** (arithmetic verified with Python, 2026-10-01).
- If only 10% of true losses get reported, real losses could be around **$6.4 billion**; at 5%, around **$12.8 billion** (same verified calculation).
- $638 million over the leap year of 2024 works out to roughly **$1.74 million lost per day**.

The takeaway is not that every Canadian is doomed. It is that fraud is a big, professional industry, which is why the scams are so polished. Knowing the scripts takes away most of their power.

## How the common scams work

### Phishing and smishing

"Phishing" is email; "smishing" is text messages. A message that looks like it comes from your bank, the CRA, or a delivery company warns about a locked account or a refund waiting for you. The link leads to a lookalike login page that harvests your credentials. The targeted version (spear phishing) cost Canadians over $67 million in 2024. The core move: **the link**. Real banks and the CRA do not send links demanding you log in.

### Interac e-transfer interception

A fraudster gets into the recipient's email account, sees the Interac e-transfer notification, clicks the deposit link, answers a weak security question, and diverts the money to a mule account. Dollar losses from e-transfer payments rose 26.1% in 2024. The fix is structural: **register for Interac auto-deposit**, which deposits transfers straight into your account with no security question to answer. Senders can help too, by using hard-to-guess questions and sharing the answer through a different channel, never in the email.

### CRA impersonation

A call or text claims to be from the Canada Revenue Agency. It threatens arrest or a frozen bank account unless you pay immediately, often by gift card, crypto, or e-transfer. The real CRA's rules, confirmed on canada.ca:

- The CRA **never** threatens arrest or uses aggressive language.
- The CRA **never** demands payment by gift card, prepaid card, crypto, or Interac e-transfer.
- The CRA **never** starts a conversation by text, WhatsApp, or Messenger. A text claiming to be the CRA is a scam.
- The CRA **never** leaves personal financial details on your voicemail.

The CRA may legitimately call about a tax debt, but you can always hang up and verify by calling the official number **1-800-959-8281** yourself.

### The bank inspector / courier scam

The phone rings and the display shows your bank's real name or number. The caller says your cards are compromised and a "bank inspector" or courier is coming to collect them. A real person then shows up at your door. Banks **never** send couriers to collect cards, PINs, or cash. This scam ran repeatedly in 2026: Durham police reported a victim losing over $12,000, a Guelph senior lost nearly $20,000 after handing cards to a fake courier, and Toronto police made an arrest after $11,000 and $14,000 shopping sprees. The rule is absolute: **no real bank collects your cards in person**.

### Romance scams

A profile on a dating app, social network, or even LinkedIn builds a relationship over weeks or months, then asks for money for an "emergency," a plane ticket, or a can't-miss investment. Canadians lost over $58 million to romance scams in 2024, with people aged 30 and up losing $42.5 million of that. Romance scams increasingly blend with fake investment pitches, where the "investment" is a fake trading site and the relationship was the sales channel.

### Job and cheque scams

Fake job postings (mystery shopper, "package processor," or an easy work-from-home gig) ask you to deposit a cheque, keep a "commission," and forward the rest by wire or e-transfer. The cheque is fake; the bank reverses it and **you** are liable for the full amount. Variations include fake LMIA offers demanding upfront fees and "courier jobs" that turn out to be cash pickups for other scams, which can make you an unknowing money mule. Real employers never ask you to cash cheques or move money through your personal account.

### Rental and roommate scams

A rental listing is far below market price, the "landlord" is conveniently out of town, and they pressure you to e-transfer a deposit before a viewing. Or a would-be roommate sends a cheque for more than the rent and asks you to return the difference: the cheque is fake. Meet landlords in person or by video, never send deposits for places you have not seen, and never return money from an overpayment before your bank confirms the original funds are legitimate.

## The 8 red flags

1. **Manufactured urgency.** "Pay within the hour or you will be arrested." Real institutions give you time.
2. **Unreversible payment demands.** Gift cards, crypto, prepaid cards, wire transfers, or e-transfers to strangers.
3. **A link in an unexpected message.** If you did not request it, do not click it. Go to the site yourself.
4. **Spoofed identity.** Caller ID and email addresses can be faked. Real CRA pages live on canada.ca or .gc.ca.
5. **Demands for secrecy.** "Don't tell your family or your bank." Honest parties want you to check with someone you trust.
6. **Too good to be true.** Outsized investment returns, effortless pay, half-price rent, a refund you never applied for.
7. **Requests for credentials.** Your SIN, passwords, PINs, or one-time login codes.
8. **Overpayment.** Sent you too much and asking for the difference back: a cheque scam.

## What to do in the first 24 hours if you got scammed

Speed limits the damage. Work through this list in order.

1. **Stop all contact with the scammer.** Do not argue, do not "verify," and do not send any more money, even if they promise to return what they took.
2. **Contact your bank immediately.** Flag the transactions and ask about reversing them. For credit cards, maximum legal liability for unauthorized use is **$50** unless the bank proves gross negligence; Visa and Mastercard also run zero-liability programs. For debit cards, you generally get your money back if you protected your PIN and reported promptly (FCAC).
3. **Report to the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre** at **1-888-495-8501** or through its online reporting system. Report even if you lost nothing: attempts help police spot patterns.
4. **Report to your local police.** You will want the file number for the bank and the credit bureaus.
5. **Place fraud alerts with both credit bureaus.** Call Equifax Canada (1-800-465-7166) and TransUnion Canada (1-800-663-9980), request a fraud alert, and check your reports for accounts you did not open.
6. **Change passwords, starting with your email.** Email is the master key to password resets. Then banking, then CRA My Account. Turn on two-factor authentication everywhere it is offered.
7. **If your SIN was exposed**, report it to Service Canada.
8. **Document everything.** Screenshots, the scammer's number and email, receipts, dates and times, all in one folder.

## Prevention habits that actually work

- **Turn on Interac auto-deposit** for every email address you receive transfers at. It removes the security question entirely, which removes the interception window.
- **Treat every unexpected message as unverified until proven otherwise.** Open your bank app directly, type canada.ca into your browser yourself, or call the number on the back of your card. Never use the number or link the message provided.
- **Report scam texts to 7726 (SPAM).** It costs nothing and helps carriers block the senders.
- **Use two-factor authentication** on banking, email, and CRA accounts, plus unique passwords. A breached email account is how e-transfer interceptions start.
- **Review your statements monthly.** Catching a small unauthorized charge early often stops a bigger one.
- **Keep the CRA's real rules handy** (above) so the panic call cannot override what you know.

## Traps: where "being careful" goes wrong

**1. Calling back the number in the message.** The scammer answers, of course. "Verifying" with a number the fraudster chose is not verification. Look the number up independently.

**2. The gift-card payment demand.** No government agency, bank, or legitimate business accepts gift cards as payment. The moment gift cards enter the conversation, it is a scam.

**3. The overpayment cheque that "cleared."** A deposit showing up in your balance is not the same as the cheque being real. Banks can reverse fraudulent cheques weeks later, and the full amount comes back out of your account. Never forward the difference until your bank confirms the funds are legitimate.

**4. The fund-recovery pitch.** Fraudsters impersonate the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre itself, promising to recover lost money for a fee. The real CAFC never demands payment, never asks for your SIN or bank details, and never promises to recover funds. Guaranteed recovery is the second scam on the same victim.
