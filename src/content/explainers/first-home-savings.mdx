## The verdict

Buying a first home in Canada has three tax-advantaged sources of down-payment money, and they stack in a fixed order. The FHSA comes first: deductible contributions going in, tax-free growth inside, tax-free withdrawals for a qualifying first home, and no repayment, ever. The Home Buyers' Plan comes second: it turns RRSP savings already built up into down-payment money today, up to **$60,000** per person, with the price that every dollar is repaid to the RRSP over 15 years. The TFSA is the overflow: flexible, tax-free withdrawals, no deduction, no repayment, no deadline pressure. A couple using the two registered levers can assemble up to **$200,000** of tax-assisted down-payment money, with the TFSA holding whatever does not fit the other two. That is the whole picture; everything below is the math that fills it in.

## The stack order

**Pillar 1: FHSA first.** For new savings, the FHSA wins at every income level. The annual room is **$8,000**, the lifetime cap is **$40,000**, and room only starts accumulating in the year the first account is opened. The deduction is worth the marginal rate on every dollar contributed: at a $60,000 income in Ontario (29.65% combined marginal: 20.5% federal plus 9.15% Ontario), each $8,000 year returns **$2,372** at tax time. Qualifying withdrawals are never repaid. The exit ramp is clean too: if the purchase never happens, the balance rolls tax-free into an RRSP or RRIF without touching RRSP room.

**Pillar 2: HBP second.** The HBP is for speed, not for savings: it unlocks up to $60,000 per person from money already inside an RRSP. Contributions must have sat in the RRSP for at least 90 days before the withdrawal, and repayments run one-fifteenth per year for 15 years, starting in the second calendar year after the withdrawal and designated on Schedule 7. (A temporary 5-year grace applied only to first withdrawals made 2022-2025; the standard schedule applies otherwise.) It stacks with the FHSA for the same home. The FHSA-plus-HBP couple ceiling is $200,000.

**Pillar 3: TFSA as overflow.** The TFSA holds what the other two cannot: savings beyond FHSA room, the 90-day seasoning window for HBP money, and anything needed with zero strings attached. No deduction, no repayment, no first-time-buyer test, no 15-year clock. It is the parking lot, and a good strategy has one.

Why the order matters: the FHSA's deduction is new money back; the HBP's deduction happened years ago when the RRSP was funded; the TFSA never had one. Filling them in any other order leaves the FHSA's deduction on the table.

## How the pieces interact at purchase time

The stack is not three separate plans; it is one pool with three taps. The CRA confirms an FHSA qualifying withdrawal and an HBP withdrawal can fund the same qualifying home, so at purchase time both registered taps open together. The TFSA covers what the other two cannot schedule: the 90-day seasoning window for RRSP money destined for the HBP, and the closing-cost overflow that arrives after the down payment is set. Gifts from immediate family land in the same pool with a gift letter and the paper trail attached. The down payment planner sequences all of it month by month, because the order money is saved in is the order it becomes usable.

## The December 31 band

FHSA room does not accrue from age 18 the way TFSA room does. It starts at zero and begins only in the year the first FHSA is opened, and unused room carries forward capped at $8,000. The calendar math: open in December 2026 with $0 contributed and 2027 opens with **$16,000** of room ($8,000 new plus $8,000 carried forward). Wait until January 2027 and 2027 opens with $8,000. One month of waiting permanently costs **$8,000 of lifetime room**, a fifth of the $40,000 cap. The account can sit at $0 while the room banks; the opening date is what matters. (Full checklist: the FHSA Dec-31 campaign page.)

## Worked example: $40,000 of FHSA room on a $600,000 home

Lucas is buying a $600,000 home in Ontario in five years and earns $60,000. The minimum down payment is tiered: 5% of the first $500,000 ($25,000) plus 10% of the remaining $100,000 ($10,000), for **$35,000**.

He opens an FHSA in 2026 and contributes the $8,000 annual max for five years. Room math: $8,000 a year for five years is $40,000, exactly the lifetime cap, and the single-year maximum ($8,000 new plus $8,000 carried forward) never binds because he contributes each year's room in full. The $40,000 covers the entire $35,000 minimum down payment, withdrawn tax-free for the qualifying purchase, with $5,000 of headroom left toward closing costs.

The deduction math: at a 29.65% combined marginal rate, each $8,000 contribution returns $2,372 at tax time, or **$11,860** across the five years. That refund money, reinvested, is the compounding the FHSA adds on top of the tax-free withdrawal.

If Lucas were buying in two years instead of five, the FHSA ramp would allow at most $16,000 of room, leaving a $19,000 gap to the $35,000 minimum. That gap is what the HBP fills: up to $60,000 from his RRSP, withdrawn tax-free, then repaid at **$4,000** a year for 15 years starting in the second year after the withdrawal. Faster timeline, same stack, different pillar doing the heavy lifting.

Math checked 2026-10-02: $25,000 + $10,000 = $35,000; 5 x $8,000 = $40,000; $8,000 x 29.65% = $2,372; $2,372 x 5 = $11,860; $60,000 / 15 = $4,000.

## Four traps that cost real money

**1. The unopened FHSA.** Every December 31 without an open FHSA deletes $8,000 of room that carry-forward can never restore, because carry-forward only accrues after opening and caps at $8,000. Cost: up to **$8,000 of lifetime tax-free room** per year of delay, each dollar of it worth the marginal rate in foregone deductions.

**2. The 90-day HBP rule.** Contributions must sit in the RRSP for 90+ days before an HBP withdrawal, or they are not eligible for the withdrawal and the expected deduction treatment breaks. Moving money into the RRSP the month before closing is the classic version. Cost: on a $10,000 contribution at a 29.65% marginal rate, up to **$2,965** of expected tax benefit at risk.

**3. The RRSP-to-FHSA transfer.** Moving money from an RRSP into an FHSA uses FHSA room but gives no new deduction; the deduction was already claimed on the RRSP side, and the transfer does not restore RRSP room. It belongs after deductible contributions are maxed, never instead of them. Cost: **$2,372** of foregone deduction value per $8,000 transferred instead of contributed fresh, at 29.65%.

**4. The missed HBP repayment.** Each year's minimum repayment (one-fifteenth of the withdrawal) must be designated as a repayment on Schedule 7; a plain RRSP contribution does not count, and the shortfall is added to taxable income for the year. Cost: a $4,000 missed repayment at 29.65% becomes **$1,186** of tax.
