## The 30-second version

Three things actually expire at year end: **charitable donations** (must be made by December 31 to claim on the 2026 return), **TFSA withdrawal timing** (a withdrawal on December 31 gives the room back January 1, 2027; one day later costs a full year of room), and **tax-loss harvesting** (sell by Tuesday, December 29, 2026, the last trading date that settles in 2026). Everything else, including TFSA room and RRSP room, carries forward and can wait.

## Move 1: Time the TFSA withdrawal to the calendar, not the mood

The TFSA has one timing quirk and it bites exactly at year end. When money is withdrawn from a TFSA, the amount does **not** come back as contribution room immediately. The CRA adds it back on **January 1 of the next calendar year** (canada.ca, "Withdrawing from a TFSA").

The worked math:

- Withdraw **$12,000 on December 31, 2026** → that $12,000 of room returns on **January 1, 2027**.
- Withdraw **$12,000 on January 1, 2027** → the same room does not return until **January 1, 2028**.

One day of timing costs a full year of contribution room. So for a large withdrawal that is planned anyway, such as funding a January bill or moving money to down-payment savings, December beats January. Conversely, anyone who already pulled money out in 2026 and was maxed out should **not re-contribute it before January 1** without confirmed spare room: same-year re-contributions of withdrawals are brand-new contributions, and the 1% per month over-contribution tax applies (see the fixing-TFSA-overcontributions explainer).

Two things this move is NOT: it is not a deadline, because the withdrawal itself can happen any time. And unused TFSA room does not expire; it carries forward indefinitely. This is purely about *when* the room that was freed up becomes usable again.

## Move 2: Donate by December 31 (and check the receipt, not just the cause)

Charitable donations must be **made by December 31, 2026** to be claimed on the 2026 tax return. The practical details matter more than the deadline itself:

- **Online donations** are dated when the payment is processed, not when the button is clicked. A donation made at 11:58 p.m. on December 31 that processes January 1 is a 2027 donation.
- **Mailed cheques** are generally treated as made on the **postmark date**, and the last week of December is a poor time to rely on the post.
- Only gifts to **qualified donees** (registered charities and the other organizations on the CRA's list) with an **official donation receipt** count. No receipt, no credit. Donation schemes, the "buy a receipt" arrangements the CRA actively audits, do not count as gifts, and claiming them buys a reassessment, not a refund.

The 2026 credit math (federal):

- **14% on the first $200** claimed. Note: this used to be 15% in every article written before 2026. The 2026 federal budget cut the lowest bracket to 14%, and the first-$200 credit rate tracks the lowest bracket. Older sources quoting 15% are stale for 2026.
- **29% on everything above $200.**
- **33%** on the above-$200 portion to the extent there is income taxed in the top 33% federal bracket (over $258,482 in 2026).

Provinces stack their own credit on top: combined federal + provincial, donations above $200 typically return **40% to 50%** of the gift, depending on province and income. A $1,000 donation from a donor in a middle bracket returns roughly $300 to $400 in combined credits.

Three power moves:

1. **Donate appreciated shares, not cash.** Transferring publicly listed securities directly to the charity means **zero capital gains tax** on the accrued gain, while still getting a donation receipt for the full fair market value. A $5,000 gift of shares that cost $2,000 saves roughly $750 in capital gains tax (at a ~50% marginal rate on the 50% inclusion) that a cash donation never would. This is the single highest-value giving move in the Canadian tax system, and December is when brokerages get backlogged on in-kind transfers, so start the paperwork by mid-December.
2. **Pool spouses' donations on one return.** Only the first $200 per claim year gets the low rate, so two spouses each claiming $150 gets the 14% rate twice, while one spouse claiming $300 gets 29% on $100 of it.
3. **Know the limits.** Donations can be claimed up to **75% of net income** for the year; anything above that (or donations left unclaimed) carries forward up to **5 years**. If 2026 income is low but 2027 will be higher, donating now and claiming later can be the better play.

The credit is non-refundable: it reduces tax owed down to zero, but it never generates a refund on its own.

## Move 3: Harvest capital losses before the settlement cutoff

Holding losers in a **non-registered** account after realizing gains this year (or in the last three) means selling the losers before year end converts paper losses into tax offsets. This move does nothing inside a TFSA or RRSP: there is no capital gains tax to offset inside registered accounts, so harvesting there is pointless.

**The cutoff is about settlement, not the trade date.** The CRA looks at the settlement date. Trades settle T+1 now, and **Tuesday, December 29, 2026 is the last trading date whose settlement lands in 2026** (Broadridge/TD "Major Canadian Tax Deadlines and Dates 2026" chart). Brokerages publish their own year-end trading deadlines in December. The brokerage's published date is the one that governs the account, and it can be earlier than December 29. Place the order a day or two before any published cutoff; a trade that settles January 2 is a 2027 loss no matter what the trade ticket says.

How the harvested loss works:

- Net capital losses first offset capital gains realized in the same year.
- Unused net capital losses carry **back up to 3 years** (claimed on Schedule 4 of the loss-year return; the CRA reassesses the prior year, so no amended return is needed) or **forward indefinitely**.
- The capital gains inclusion rate is **50%** for 2026 (the proposed increase to 66.67% was cancelled March 21, 2025, and the 50% rate is back to stay). A $4,000 harvested loss is therefore worth about $1,000 in tax savings at a 50% marginal rate. That is real money, but not a windfall, and not worth paying meaningful trading costs to manufacture.

**The 30-day trap: the superficial loss rule.** If the seller, a spouse, or a corporation or trust affiliated with the seller buys the **same or identical property** in the 30 days *before or after* the sale (a 61-day window including the sale day) and still holds it at the end of the window, the CRA denies the loss entirely: it is deemed to be nil. The denied loss is not destroyed, though. It gets added to the adjusted cost base of the repurchased property, so it resurfaces when that property is eventually sold. Two common ways people trip this:

- Selling the loser and immediately rebuying it in a **TFSA or RRSP**. The repurchase triggers the rule just the same as rebuying in the taxable account.
- Transferring a losing investment **in-kind** from a taxable account into a TFSA or RRSP at a loss. The Income Tax Act denies that loss outright (a deemed disposition at a loss on transfer to a registered account is not claimable).

To keep exposure to the position, wait out the full 30 days after the sale before rebuying, or buy a *similar but not identical* security (a different ETF tracking the same index is the standard approach).

## What does NOT expire on December 31

Worth saying plainly, because the year-end marketing noise blurs it:

- **RRSP room** carries forward indefinitely. And the RRSP *contribution deadline* for the 2026 tax year is not December 31 at all: contributions in the first 60 days of 2027 (up to **March 1, 2027**) can be deducted on the 2026 return. That is the single most misunderstood date in Canadian personal tax, and it is why the RRSP push belongs in February, not December.
- **Unused TFSA room** never expires. The only December-sensitive TFSA mechanic is withdrawal timing (Move 1).

## The December checklist

1. **By mid-December:** start any in-kind share donations (brokerages bottleneck) and confirm the brokerage's published year-end trading deadline. The LoonieLogic promo-expiry calendar tracks other year-end dates worth checking at the same time.
2. **By December 29:** execute any tax-loss sales in non-registered accounts, watching the 30-day repurchase rule.
3. **By December 31:** make all charitable donations (online: confirm the payment processed, not just submitted); make any planned TFSA withdrawals so the room returns January 1, 2027; confirm donation receipts show the charity's registration number.
4. **January:** recalculate TFSA room with the 2026 withdrawals added back; file donations and carryback claims (Schedule 4) at filing time; watch for the 2027 TFSA limit announcement (around November to December 2026).
