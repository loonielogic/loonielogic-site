On a gray morning, the loon tucks five shiny loonies into a tiny pouch and waddles to the shore market. The first stall sells warm boots. Her feet are cold. "I NEED those," she says, and the vendor nods.

The second stall sells a glittering mirror that catches the sun. "I want that," she says, and smiles, and keeps her pouch closed. The third stall sells berries. Her stomach growls. "Those I need too," she says.

At the end of the market, the loon counts what is left: two loonies. She holds them up like a trophy. "Wants can wait," she says. "Needs cannot."

Here is the trick she learned, and it works for people too. A need is something you must have to be healthy and safe: food, a warm coat, a roof, medicine. A want is something that is nice or fun to have: the newest game, a fancy drink, that mirror. Both are fine to have. The difference is which one gets your money FIRST.

Needs and wants can also change places. Breakfast before school is a need. A chocolate bar at midnight is a want. A bus ticket to get to practice is a need; the limited-edition jersey is a want. Some things sit in the middle, and that is normal. The question that sorts them is simple: "If I did not buy this, would I be okay?" If yes, it is a want. If no, it is a need.

Why does this matter? Because money is limited. Everyone has a pile that is only so tall, kids included. When needs get paid first, there is always money for the important stuff. When wants sneak to the front of the line, the pile runs out before the boots get bought. Sorting needs from wants is the whole game. It is the first skill behind saving, and it never stops being useful.
