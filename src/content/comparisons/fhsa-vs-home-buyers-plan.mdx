## The 30-second version

**For new money, the FHSA wins in every income bracket.** It gives a tax deduction now AND a tax-free withdrawal at purchase. The Home Buyers' Plan is a loan from your own RRSP: useful when you need scale fast from money you already have, but every dollar must be repaid, and each repayment eats future RRSP room without giving you a new deduction. **Use the FHSA first, the HBP second, and stack both.** A couple can reach **$200,000** in tax-assisted down payment money.

## Side-by-side: what each one actually is

| | FHSA (First Home Savings Account) | HBP (Home Buyers' Plan) |
|---|---|---|
| **What it is** | A registered account: deduct contributions now, withdraw tax-free for your first home | A loan to yourself from your existing RRSP: withdraw tax-free, repay it all |
| **Contribution limit** | $8,000 per year, $40,000 lifetime | No limit: you withdraw what's already in your RRSP, up to the cap |
| **Withdrawal cap** | $40,000 lifetime | $60,000 per person (raised from $35,000 on Apr 16, 2024) |
| **Deduction on the way in** | Yes, at your marginal tax rate | No: the deduction happened when you contributed to the RRSP |
| **Tax on a qualifying withdrawal** | $0 | $0 |
| **Repayment** | None. The money is yours. | Full amount over 15 years, minimum 1/15 per year |
| **Repayment start** | Not applicable | The 2nd year after the withdrawal year. A temporary 5-year grace applied only to first withdrawals made Jan 1, 2022 to Dec 31, 2025; first withdrawals in 2026 and later use the standard 2nd-year schedule |
| **If you miss a repayment** | Not applicable | The shortfall is added to your taxable income that year |
| **Effect on future RRSP room** | None: FHSA room is separate | Repayments use up RRSP contribution room and give NO new deduction |
| **When room starts** | Only when you open the account: every year you wait is $8,000 you can never get back | Depends on the RRSP savings you already have |
| **Max in one year** | $16,000 (one year of carry-forward, capped at $8,000) | Limited by what's in your RRSP |
| **90-day rule** | No | Yes: contributions must sit in the RRSP 90+ days before an HBP withdrawal |
| **First-time-buyer test** | Did not live in a home you or your spouse owned, in the year you open the account or the 4 prior years | Same 4-year test, measured at withdrawal time |
| **Account expiry** | Closes at 15 years or the year you turn 71, whichever comes first | No expiry: the RRSP keeps living |
| **If you don't buy** | Transfer to your RRSP/RRIF tax-free (uses no RRSP room) or withdraw and pay tax | Unused money must go back to the RRSP or be included in income |
| **Couple stacking** | $40,000 per eligible partner | $60,000 per eligible partner |

One line that surprises people: you can use BOTH for the same home. The CRA confirms you may make an FHSA qualifying withdrawal and an HBP withdrawal for the same purchase.

## The math behind the verdict

The FHSA's core advantage is arithmetic, not opinion. Its deduction is worth your marginal tax rate times your contribution. Ontario 2026 marginal rates, combining the verified federal and Ontario bracket tables:

### Five years of max FHSA contributions ($40,000 total)

| Income | Ontario marginal rate | Tax saved per $8,000 year | Saved over 5 years |
|---|---|---|---|
| $40,000 | 19.05% | $1,524 | $7,620 |
| $60,000 | 29.65% | $2,372 | $11,860 |

Higher incomes follow the same shape (deduction = contribution x your marginal rate); find your own rate with the [income tax calculator](/calculators/income-tax-calculator).

That $7,620 to $11,860 in tax savings is money the HBP simply cannot provide, because the HBP's deduction happened years ago when the RRSP was funded. The HBP's equivalent benefit is $0. The FHSA is also the only one of the two where the withdrawal never has to come back.

### The HBP's hidden cost: roughly $17,800

Take the full $60,000 out under the HBP. The minimum repayment is $4,000 a year for 15 years. Each repayment is a contribution to your RRSP that uses up contribution room but gives you **no new deduction**: it must be designated as a repayment on Schedule 7 of your tax return.

For a steady RRSP contributor whose $4,000 a year would otherwise have been a fresh deductible contribution: at a 29.65% marginal rate, the deduction you never get on each $4,000 repayment-year contribution is 0.2965 x $4,000 = $1,186 a year, or **$17,790 over the 15 years**. That is the real price of HBP money.

(Caveat, stated plainly: this cost only bites if you would have had the RRSP room and made the contributions anyway. If you wouldn't have saved that money at all, the HBP's cost is just the lost growth, not lost deductions.)

### The speed math: when the FHSA alone can't do it

The FHSA is room-capped per year; the HBP is instant if the RRSP exists:

- **Buying in about a year, opening an FHSA today:** you get $8,000 of room this year, plus $8,000 next January 1. That's roughly $16,000 before a mid-next-year purchase. That's the ceiling.
- **Same buyer with $45,000 already in an RRSP:** the HBP unlocks up to $45,000 more, immediately. Total accessible: about $61,000 (FHSA + HBP) versus $45,000 with the HBP alone or $16,000 with the FHSA alone.
- **Buying in 5+ years:** fund the FHSA first every year to the full $40,000, no contest. Extra savings after that go to RRSP money destined for the HBP, or a TFSA for flexibility.

## When the HBP wins instead

The FHSA is the default, but the HBP is the right first call in four situations:

1. **You are buying soon and have an RRSP already.** The FHSA cannot manufacture five years of room in twelve months. The HBP turns savings you already have into down payment today.
2. **Your FHSA room is gone.** Used the $40,000 lifetime cap, or your account aged out at 15 years / age 71. The HBP rides your RRSP's rules with no such cap.
3. **You qualify under the relationship-breakdown exception.** After a separation of 90+ days within the 4-year window, the HBP's eligibility rules can admit you even when the FHSA's opening test fails you.
4. **You are buying for a disabled relative.** The HBP has no first-time-buyer condition at all; the FHSA is for your own home. Different tools, different buyers.

## The expensive mistakes (the ways Canadians actually lose money here)

1. **Waiting to open the FHSA.** Contribution room starts at account opening, not at age 18 or tax-filing. A $0-balance account still banks this year's $8,000. Every year you wait is room you never recover.
2. **The 90-day HBP trap.** Contributions must sit in the RRSP for 90+ days before an HBP withdrawal, or they can lose deductibility. Plan contributions three months ahead of the withdrawal.
3. **The Schedule 7 trap.** HBP repayments must be designated as repayments on Schedule 7. A plain RRSP contribution does not count automatically: miss the designation and the minimum repayment gets added to your income.
4. **The RRSP-to-FHSA transfer trap.** Moving money from your RRSP into your FHSA uses FHSA room but is NOT deductible. Do it only after maxing your deductible contributions, never instead of them.

## The 60-second quiz: your answer

Work top to bottom. The first question that applies to you ends the quiz.

1. **Buying within ~18 months AND have $25,000+ sitting in your RRSP?** Yes → the **HBP first**. The FHSA cannot manufacture five years of room in twelve months; the HBP turns savings you already have into a down payment today. (Still open an FHSA today to start its clock.)
2. **Is your FHSA room already maxed ($40,000 lifetime), or did your FHSA age out (15 years / age 71)?** Yes → the **HBP** is what's left. Your RRSP has no lifetime cap, so the HBP becomes the default tool.
3. **Is your purchase 2+ years away?** Yes → fund the **FHSA first, $8,000 a year**. At every income level the deduction plus tax-free withdrawal beats everything else. Anything extra after that goes to RRSP money destined for the HBP, or a TFSA for flexibility.
4. **Do you fail the 4-year first-time-buyer test but qualify under the relationship-breakdown exception?** Yes → the **HBP**. The exception's eligibility rules can admit you where the FHSA's opening test fails.
5. **Is the purchase for a disabled relative's home, not your own?** Yes → the **HBP only**. The HBP has no first-time-buyer condition; the FHSA is for your own home. Different buyers, different tools.
6. **Need more than $40,000 per person before your purchase date?** Yes → **stack both**: FHSA to its cap, then HBP on top. The CRA confirms you may make an FHSA qualifying withdrawal and an HBP withdrawal for the same home. A couple can reach **$200,000**.

No to all of the above, or genuinely unsure? **Default to the FHSA.** It is the only one of the two that pays you on the way in AND never asks for the money back, and every year you wait to open it is $8,000 of room gone forever.

## What to do: the 4-rule sequence

1. **Open an FHSA today, even with nothing to contribute yet.** Room starts at opening.
2. **Fund the FHSA first every year** ($8,000 a year) until you hit $40,000 or buy. At every income level, the deduction plus tax-free withdrawal makes this the highest-return move.
3. **Top up with the HBP** for anything the FHSA can't cover before your purchase date. It is the only way to pull more than $16,000 a year (per person) toward a near-term purchase, and it works on money you already have.
4. **Stack both, per person, per partner.** Two eligible partners: 2 × ($40,000 FHSA + $60,000 HBP) = **$200,000** in tax-assisted down payment.
